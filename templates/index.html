Question 1

Where is growth coming from, and which markets are losing ground?

Why it matters

The sales team is small: nine employees cover 21 countries. Knowing which markets drive growth and which are shrinking tells the sales manager where to spend visits and where to step in.

Approach

Revenue on each order line is UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount). Orders are assigned to the customer's country. The data ends on 6 May 1998, so a full-year comparison would be biased. Instead I compare the same four months in both years (January–April 1997 vs January–April 1998), which also removes seasonal effects.

$fig_growth

Main finding

Revenue for January–April rose from $rev97 to $rev98 ($growth_pct). Growth is concentrated: $g1_name (+$g1_change), $g2_name (+$g2_change) and $g3_name (+$g3_change) together account for $top3_growth_share of the increase. $n_decliners markets shrank: $decliner_list. Each of these declines comes from one or two accounts. In Canada, Mère Paillarde stopped ordering entirely (see Question 2). In Denmark, Simons bistro placed no orders between January and April 1998.

Business implication

Keep investing in the three largest growth markets, but treat the declining markets as account problems, not country problems. A single call to the lapsed Canadian and Danish accounts is cheaper than a market-level campaign. Because small markets depend on one or two customers, every account there should have a named owner.
Question 2

How dependent are we on a few customers, and are any key accounts slipping away?

Why it matters

If a small number of customers produce most of the revenue, losing even one of them is a material risk. Spotting customers who have gone quiet, before they are officially lost, gives sales a chance to win them back.

Approach

I ranked customers by lifetime revenue and plotted the cumulative share (a Pareto curve). For churn risk, I measured each customer's own ordering rhythm: the average number of days between their first and last order, divided by the number of gaps. A customer is flagged as lapsed if they have at least 3 orders and their silence as of 6 May 1998 is more than twice their usual gap. Comparing each customer with their own history avoids flagging naturally infrequent buyers.

Main finding

Revenue is concentrated: the top 3 customers produce $top3_share of revenue, the top 10 produce $top10_share, and $n_for_80 of $n_customers customers account for 80%. $n_lapsed customers meet the lapsed rule, with $lapsed_total in combined lifetime revenue. The most serious case is $lapsed_top_name, the #$lapsed_top_rank customer ($lapsed_top_rev). It usually orders every $lapsed_top_gap days but has been silent for $lapsed_top_silent days.

Business implication

Assign senior coverage to the top 10 accounts and track days since each account's last order against its usual gap every month. That costs little and gives early warning. Mère Paillarde and Blondel père et fils should be called first. Together they make up most of the flagged revenue, and Mère Paillarde alone explains Canada's decline in Question 1.

Caveat: the data cannot tell a lost customer from one that is simply pausing. The flag is a reason to call, not proof of churn.

Question 3

Do deeper discounts actually win bigger orders?

Why it matters

Northwind gave away $total_discount in discounts ($discount_of_gross of gross sales) across $n_discount_lines order lines. Discounts that do not change customer behaviour are lost margin.

Approach

Quantities differ widely by product (people buy more tea than caviar), so comparing raw order sizes would mostly measure product mix. Instead, each order line's quantity is divided by the average undiscounted quantity for the same product. A value of 1.0 means a normal-sized order for that product. I then compared this relative quantity across discount levels. $n_odd_discounts lines with unusual 1–6% discounts were excluded; they are too few to analyse.

$fig_discount
$discount_table

Two further checks test whether the pattern holds up. (1) Among discounted lines only, I fitted relative quantity against discount depth, with a bootstrap 95% interval for the slope. (2) Because larger customers may simply receive more discounts, I compared each customer with themselves: for customers who bought at two different discount bands, I compared their average relative quantity in each band.

Main finding

Deep discounts buy nothing extra. Among discounted lines, each additional point of discount changes relative quantity by $slope (95% CI $slope_ci), which is effectively zero. The $wc_deep_n customers who received both 5–10% and 15–25% discounts placed orders of the same size at either depth (median difference $wc_deep_diff; deeper orders were larger for only $wc_deep_share of them). Yet discounts of 15% or more account for $deep_discount_share of all discount dollars.

5% and 10% cannot be told apart. Mean relative quantity is $rq5 at 5% (95% CI $ci5) and $rq10 at 10% (95% CI $ci10). Within the $wc_5v10_n customers who received both, the median difference is $wc_5v10_diff.

Even the benefit of a small discount is uncertain. Across all lines, discounted orders look about 30% larger than undiscounted ones. Within the same customer the gap shrinks to a median of $wc_any_diff, and only $wc_any_share of $wc_any_n customers order more when discounted. Most of the raw gap reflects which customers get discounts, not what the discount does.

Business implication

Now: cap discounts at 10% and require manager approval above that. This removes the 15–25% tiers, where the evidence of no extra volume is strongest. At historical order sizes it would have kept $cap10_savings ($cap10_savings_pct of revenue).

Next: run a simple test before going lower. Randomly offer 5% or 10% on new quotes for a quarter and compare order sizes and reorder rates. A 5% cap would keep a further $cap_gap, but the historical data cannot show whether 5% loses volume compared with 10%, so that step should wait for the test.

Caveat: this is observational data. Reps may give discounts because an order is large (reverse causality), and discounts may go to customers who would have ordered more anyway (selection). The within-customer comparison reduces but does not remove these effects. The savings figures assume order sizes stay the same.

Question 4

Why do orders arrive late, and is the shipper to blame?

Why it matters

Late deliveries damage relationships with exactly the grocers and restaurants that reorder most often. To fix the problem, management first needs to know where it comes from: the carrier, or the promise made when the order was taken.

Approach

An order counts as late if it shipped after its RequiredDate. Of $n_shipped shipped orders, $n_late were late ($late_overall). $n_unshipped orders placed in the last weeks of the data had not yet shipped and are excluded. Every order carries one of three promised windows: 14, 28 or 42 days. I compared late rates by window and by shipper, with 95% confidence intervals because the number of late orders is small.

$fig_late
$fig_lead

Main finding

The promised window, not the shipper, drives lateness. Orders with a 14-day deadline are late $late14 of the time (95% CI $late14_lo–$late14_hi), compared with $late28 for 28-day orders. They are only $n14_share of orders but $rush_late_share of all late orders. The cause is that rush orders are not handled any faster: median time to ship is $med14 days for 14-day orders and $med28 days for 28-day orders, and the averages are $avg14 vs $avg28. Late rates by shipper range only from $ship_min to $ship_max, and their confidence intervals overlap.

Business implication

Switching carriers would not fix this. Instead, (1) flag orders with a 14-day deadline in the warehouse so they are picked first, and (2) have sales stop promising 14 days unless stock is confirmed. The long tail of orders taking 20 to 37 days to ship affects all windows, which points to stock shortages (see Question 5) rather than transit time.
Question 5

Which products are about to run out, and where is cash tied up in slow stock?

Why it matters

A stockout on a best-seller loses sales immediately and can push customers to a competitor. Overstock ties up cash in perishable food. Both problems can be fixed with purchasing decisions.

Approach

For each active product I calculated its recent sales rate (average units per month over the last $inv_months full months, Nov 1997 – Apr 1998) and its months of cover: (units in stock + units on order) ÷ units per month. Products with less than 1 month of cover are flagged as stockout risks. Products with more than 3 months of stock on hand are flagged as overstocked. Discontinued products are excluded.

Main finding

$n_at_risk of $n_active active products have less than one month of cover, and these products earned $at_risk_rev_share of recent revenue. $no_order_at_risk of them have nothing on order. The largest exposure is $top_risk_name: $top_risk_rev of revenue in six months and about $top_risk_rate units sold per month, with $top_risk_stock units in stock and none on order. Meanwhile, $n_overstock slow sellers have more than three months of stock.

Business implication

Reorder levels are out of date: many fast sellers have reorder levels of 0–15 units while selling 45–110 units a month. Purchasing should reset reorder levels to at least one month of recent demand, starting with the products in the table, and stop reordering the overstocked slow sellers until their stock runs down.

Caveat: the stock figures are a single snapshot with no date. They are treated here as the position at the end of the order data. If the snapshot is older, the cover estimates would change.
