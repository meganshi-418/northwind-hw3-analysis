Question 1

Where is growth coming from, and which markets are losing ground?

Why it matters

The sales team is small: nine employees cover 21 countries. Knowing which markets drive growth and which are shrinking tells the sales manager where to spend visits and where to step in.

Approach

Revenue on each order line is UnitPrice × Quantity × (1 − Discount). Orders are assigned to the customer's country. The data ends on 6 May 1998, so a full-year comparison would be biased. Instead I compare the same four months in both years (January–April 1997 vs January–April 1998), which also removes seasonal effects.

Main finding

Revenue for January–April rose from $191,322 to $422,290 (121%). Growth is concentrated: USA (+$53,190), Germany (+$47,407) and Brazil (+$31,712) together account for 57% of the increase. 4 markets shrank: Denmark (-$9,422), Canada (-$3,685), Mexico (-$3,080), Finland (-$1,875). Each of these declines comes from one or two accounts. In Canada, Mère Paillarde stopped ordering entirely (see Question 2). In Denmark, Simons bistro placed no orders between January and April 1998.

Business implication

Keep investing in the three largest growth markets, but treat the declining markets as account problems, not country problems. A single call to the lapsed Canadian and Danish accounts is cheaper than a market-level campaign. Because small markets depend on one or two customers, every account there should have a named owner.
Question 2

How dependent are we on a few customers, and are any key accounts slipping away?

Why it matters

If a small number of customers produce most of the revenue, losing even one of them is a material risk. Spotting customers who have gone quiet, before they are officially lost, gives sales a chance to win them back.

Approach

I ranked customers by lifetime revenue and plotted the cumulative share (a Pareto curve). For churn risk, I measured each customer's own ordering rhythm: the average number of days between their first and last order, divided by the number of gaps. A customer is flagged as lapsed if they have at least 3 orders and their silence as of 6 May 1998 is more than twice their usual gap. Comparing each customer with their own history avoids flagging naturally infrequent buyers.

Revenue is concentrated: the top 3 customers produce 25% of revenue, the top 10 produce 45%, and 34 of 89 customers account for 80%. 5 customers meet the lapsed rule, with $56,569 in combined lifetime revenue. The most serious case is Mère Paillarde, the #9 customer ($28,872). It usually orders every 32 days but has been silent for 188 days.

Business implication

Assign senior coverage to the top 10 accounts and track days since each account's last order against its usual gap every month. That costs little and gives early warning. Mère Paillarde and Blondel père et fils should be called first. Together they make up most of the flagged revenue, and Mère Paillarde alone explains Canada's decline in Question 1.

Caveat: the data cannot tell a lost customer from one that is simply pausing. The flag is a reason to call, not proof of churn.

Question 3

Do deeper discounts actually win bigger orders?

Why it matters

Northwind gave away $88,666 in discounts (6.5% of gross sales) across 838 order lines. Discounts that do not change customer behaviour are lost margin.

Approach

Quantities differ widely by product (people buy more tea than caviar), so comparing raw order sizes would mostly measure product mix. Instead, each order line's quantity is divided by the average undiscounted quantity for the same product. A value of 1.0 means a normal-sized order for that product. I then compared this relative quantity across discount levels. 8 lines with unusual 1–6% discounts were excluded; they are too few to analyse.

Main finding

Discounted lines are larger: at 5% off, the mean relative quantity is 1.33, compared with 1.00 for undiscounted lines. However, deeper discounts add almost nothing on top of that. At 10% the mean is 1.26, and at 25% it is 1.41. The box plots for 5% to 25% overlap almost completely. Yet discounts of 15% or more account for 80% of all discount dollars.

Business implication

Make 5% the standard discount and require manager approval for anything higher. If order sizes stayed the same, capping all discounts at 5% would have kept $58,484 (4.6% of revenue). The flat pattern from 5% to 25% suggests sizes would not fall much.

Caveat: this is observational data. Sales reps may give discounts because an order is large (a volume discount), not the other way round. A controlled test, for example offering 5% to half of new quotes and 15% to the other half, would settle the question before rolling out the policy.

Question 4

Why do orders arrive late, and is the shipper to blame?

Why it matters

Late deliveries damage relationships with exactly the grocers and restaurants that reorder most often. To fix the problem, management first needs to know where it comes from: the carrier, or the promise made when the order was taken.

Approach

An order counts as late if it shipped after its RequiredDate. Of 809 shipped orders, 37 were late (4.6%). 21 orders placed in the last weeks of the data had not yet shipped and are excluded. Every order carries one of three promised windows: 14, 28 or 42 days. I compared late rates by window and by shipper, with 95% confidence intervals because the number of late orders is small.

Main finding

The promised window, not the shipper, drives lateness. Orders with a 14-day deadline are late 17.6% of the time (95% CI 10%–28%), compared with 3.7% for 28-day orders. They are only 8% of orders but 32% of all late orders. The cause is that rush orders are not handled any faster: median time to ship is 7.5 days for 14-day orders and 7.0 days for 28-day orders, and the averages are 10.0 vs 8.3. Late rates by shipper range only from 3.6% to 5.1%, and their confidence intervals overlap.

Business implication

Switching carriers would not fix this. Instead, (1) flag orders with a 14-day deadline in the warehouse so they are picked first, and (2) have sales stop promising 14 days unless stock is confirmed. The long tail of orders taking 20 to 37 days to ship affects all windows, which points to stock shortages (see Question 5) rather than transit time.
Question 5

Which products are about to run out, and where is cash tied up in slow stock?

Why it matters

A stockout on a best-seller loses sales immediately and can push customers to a competitor. Overstock ties up cash in perishable food. Both problems can be fixed with purchasing decisions.

Approach

For each active product I calculated its recent sales rate (average units per month over the last 6 full months, Nov 1997 – Apr 1998) and its months of cover: (units in stock + units on order) ÷ units per month. Products with less than 1 month of cover are flagged as stockout risks. Products with more than 3 months of stock on hand are flagged as overstocked. Discontinued products are excluded.

Main finding

29 of 69 active products have less than one month of cover, and these products earned 71% of recent revenue. 22 of them have nothing on order. The largest exposure is Côte de Blaye: $71,277 of revenue in six months and about 46 units sold per month, with 17 units in stock and none on order. Meanwhile, 10 slow sellers have more than three months of stock.

Business implication

Reorder levels are out of date: many fast sellers have reorder levels of 0–15 units while selling 45–110 units a month. Purchasing should reset reorder levels to at least one month of recent demand, starting with the products in the table, and stop reordering the overstocked slow sellers until their stock runs down.

Caveat: the stock figures are a single snapshot with no date. They are treated here as the position at the end of the order data. If the snapshot is older, the cover estimates would change.
